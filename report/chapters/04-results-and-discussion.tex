This chapter answers the research questions in the order an examiner would ask them.
Does the data carry the brain signal it should? Is the published accuracy right? Do
neural networks change the picture? How much practice does a new patient need? Can the
sixth finger be a command of its own? What did the exploratory analyses add? Does the
demonstrator work? Each section opens with its answer in one sentence. The chapter then
discusses what the answers mean and where they stop. Unless stated otherwise, results are
for the pre-therapy session of the \nPre{} patients, given as the mean over patients with
a 95\,\% confidence interval (CI), and accuracy means balanced accuracy.

\section{Does the Data Carry the Expected Signal?}\label{sec:res-erd}

\textbf{Answer: yes.} Imagining either movement weakened the mu rhythm over the motor
cortex in almost every patient.

Figure~\ref{fig:erd} shows the mu rhythm's power over the hand area (electrode C3, on the
side that controls the weak hand) as a change from the second before the cue. Soon after the
cue it fell, and it stayed low for the whole imagery period. Averaged over
the analysis window, its power changed by \pct{\edaErdMuCThreeAhMean} for the hand and
\pct{\edaErdMuCThreeSfMean} for the sixth finger. The fall was present for the sixth finger
in \edaErdMuCThreeSfNegative{} of \edaErdMuCThreeN{} patients and for the hand in
\edaErdMuCThreeAhNegative. The sixth finger's fall was the larger in
\edaErdMuCThreeSfMoreNegative{} patients, but the difference was not significant
(exploratory Wilcoxon test, $p = \edaErdMuCThreeSfMinusAhP$). The weakening appeared on
both sides of the head, a pattern also reported for this group of
patients~\cite{wang2024tnsre}.

\section{Is the Published Accuracy Right? (RQ1)}\label{sec:res-leak}

\textbf{Answer: no.} Our rebuild of the authors' own recipe scored
\pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaPaperReplicaMean} the published way, against the 85 to 86\,\% the
dataset paper reports, and \pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaCvLeakfreeMean} once no test trial could
shape its first step. The standard method fell from \pct{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMean} to
\pct{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeMean}.

\subsection{The leak}
With CSP+LDA on hand against sixth finger, fitting CSP on all windows before the
cross-validation, as the published code does, gave \pct{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMean}
(\ci{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaCiLo}{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaCiHi}). Fitting it inside each
training fold of the same 100 folds gave \pct{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeMean}
(\ci{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeCiLo}{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeCiHi}). The difference,
\rqOneHOneDiff{} points (\cipts{\rqOneHOneDiffCiLo}{\rqOneHOneDiffCiHi}), is the leak.
It was positive for all \rqOneHOnePositive{} patients, from \rqOneHOneDiffMin{} to
\rqOneHOneDiffMax{} points (Wilcoxon $W = \rqOneHOneW$, $p = \rqOneHOneP$, $r =
\rqOneHOneR$). \textbf{H1 is \rqOneHOneCategory.} It held without the two development
patients (\rqOneHOneNoDevDiff{} points) and was repeated on the post-therapy session
(\rqOneHOnePostDiff{} points, $n = \rqOneHOnePostN$, $p = \rqOneHOnePostP$; the same
with sub-21 and sub-22 averaged, $n = \rqOneHOnePostLinkedN$). Leakage-free
cross-validation can itself fall below chance when there is little signal:
\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeNBelowChance{} of the \nPre{} patients scored under 50\,\% with it
(the lowest \pct{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeMin}), and MDM averaged
\pct{\rqOneMdmCvLeakfreeMean}, with \rqOneMdmCvLeakfreeNBelowChance{} patients under 50\,\%.
We therefore also measured the leak against the two run-wise splits, which learn from
whole runs: it was \rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMinusLoroDiff{} points against leaving one run
out (\cipts{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMinusLoroDiffCiLo}{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMinusLoroDiffCiHi})
and \rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMinusChronoDiff{} points against the chronological split
(\cipts{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMinusChronoDiffCiLo}{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMinusChronoDiffCiHi};
both descriptive, without a test). Figure~\ref{fig:leakslope} shows every patient.

\subsection{Comparison with the published number}
The replica of the authors' recipe, run their way, scored \pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaAcc}
plain accuracy (\pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaGroupBAcc} and \pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaGroupTAcc}
in the two groups), above the published \pct{\rqOnePublishedGroupBCspLda} and
\pct{\rqOnePublishedGroupTCspLda}. We therefore reproduce the direction of the leak, not
the published number itself, and the \pct{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMean} of our standard
CSP+LDA pipeline is not the authors' pipeline either. Details that neither the paper nor the code
fixes exactly, such as how the feature is averaged, the folds and the software, could
each move the leaky value. Under every leakage-free protocol the same recipe stayed between
\pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaLoroMean} and \pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaCvLeakfreeMean}.

\subsection{The same mistake elsewhere}
The leak is a property of the procedure, not of these patients (Figure~\ref{fig:elsewhere}).
On left- against right-hand imagery of healthy people, the same code inflated CSP+LDA by
\rqOneBnciHOneBDiff{} points on BCI Competition~IV~2a ($n = \rqOneBnciHOneBN$,
$p = \rqOneBnciHOneBP$) and by \rqOneChoHOneBDiff{} points on the Cho dataset
($n = \rqOneChoHOneBN$, $p = \rqOneChoHOneBP$). \textbf{H1b is supported on both.} A
separate pre-registered side study (RQ5) found the same kind of mistake in depression
detection from resting EEG, where splitting a person's recording across training and test
inflated accuracy by \rqFiveMumtazBpLrInflation{} to \rqFiveMumtazTsLrInflation{} points on
one public dataset~\cite{mumtaz2016data} and by \rqFiveModmaBpLrInflation{} to
\rqFiveModmaTsLrInflation{} on another~\cite{cai2022modma}.

\subsection{What the data does support}
The same leakage-free tests on the other two tasks tell a different story. Detecting
sixth-finger imagery against rest reached \pct{\rqOneSfRestLoroMeanMin} to
\pct{\rqOneSfRestLoroMeanMax} across the five classical methods (leave one run out), with
every patient above chance for every method. The three-class task reached
\pct{\rqOneRestAhSfLoroMeanMin} to \pct{\rqOneRestAhSfLoroMeanMax}, where guessing gives
33.3\,\%. Imagery against rest is easy to read; hand against sixth finger is not.

\section{Do Neural Networks Change the Picture?}\label{sec:res-deep}

\textbf{Answer: a little, not fundamentally.} These comparisons are descriptive and were
not part of the frozen hypotheses.

Trained on one patient's data and tested on a held-out run, EEGNet reached
\pct{\dlAhSfLoroEegnetScratchMean} on hand against sixth finger,
\pct{\dlRestAhSfLoroEegnetScratchMean} on three classes and
\pct{\dlSfRestLoroEegnetScratchMean} on sixth finger against rest
(Figure~\ref{fig:dl}). That is \dlAhSfLoroEegnetScratchVsCspLdaDiff{} and
\dlRestAhSfLoroEegnetScratchVsCspLdaDiff{} points above CSP+LDA on the two imagery tasks
($p = \dlAhSfLoroEegnetScratchVsCspLdaP$ and $\dlRestAhSfLoroEegnetScratchVsCspLdaP$) and
level on the switch. A network pre-trained on the other patients and applied with no
labelled trial from the patient (zero-shot) reached \pct{\dlZeroShotAhSfMean},
\pct{\dlZeroShotRestAhSfMean} and \pct{\dlZeroShotSfRestMean}. Retraining with another
random seed moved a patient's score by \dlEegnetScratchSeedSdMean{} points on average, a
reminder that single-patient differences of a few points are noise.

\section{How Much Practice Does a New Patient Need? (RQ2)}\label{sec:res-calib}

\textbf{Answer: with the one transfer configuration we tested, learning from other
patients gave a fast start almost at once and then stayed nearly flat; with the whole
practice pool the model trained from scratch ended numerically higher, a difference we
did not test.}

Figure~\ref{fig:setup} shows the main result for three choices (rest, hand, sixth finger).
With one practice trial of each movement, about \rqTwoMinutesKOne{} minutes, re-centred
transfer with a target weight of \rqTwoHparamRctTslrWeight{} reached
\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKOneMean}
(\ci{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKOneCiLo}{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKOneCiHi}); with no labelled
trial at all, re-centred on the rest periods alone, it reached
\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKZeroMean}. The same classifier trained from scratch on the
four windows of one trial each, two of them rest, reached \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrKOneMean}.
It needed ten trials of each, about \rqTwoMinutesKTen{} minutes (\rqTwoMinutesRecordedKTen{}
at the recorded trial length), to reach the same \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrKTenMean}. After
that the transfer curve stayed almost flat, ending at \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKAllMean}
with the whole practice pool, while the model trained from scratch kept rising to
\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrKAllMean}. That whole-pool difference,
\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrMinusRctTslrKAllDiff{} points
(\cipts{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrMinusRctTslrKAllDiffCiLo}{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrMinusRctTslrKAllDiffCiHi};
\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrMinusRctTslrKAllBetter{} patients higher from scratch,
\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrMinusRctTslrKAllWorse{} with transfer), was not tested, and its interval
includes zero.

\textbf{The confirmatory tests.} Over budgets of one to ten trials, the area under the
learning curve was larger with transfer for the typical patient (median difference
\rqTwoHTwoAMedianDiff, mean \rqTwoHTwoADiff, CI \rqTwoHTwoADiffCiLo{} to
\rqTwoHTwoADiffCiHi; $W = \rqTwoHTwoAW$, $p = \rqTwoHTwoAP$, $r = \rqTwoHTwoAR$).
\textbf{H2a is \rqTwoHTwoACategory.} Much of this comes from budgets at which the model trained
from scratch has very few windows: transfer with no labelled trial already matched it at
five trials of each movement (\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrKFiveMean}). The advantage at five
trials was larger than with the whole pool, by \rqTwoHTwoBPointsDiff{} points
(\cipts{\rqTwoHTwoBPointsDiffCiLo}{\rqTwoHTwoBPointsDiffCiHi}; $W = \rqTwoHTwoBW$,
$p = \rqTwoHTwoBP$, $r = \rqTwoHTwoBR$): \textbf{H2b is \rqTwoHTwoBCategory}, which is the
levelling-off in statistical form. Budget by budget, transfer was \rqTwoAdvantageKOneDiff{}
points ahead at one trial (Holm-adjusted $p = \rqTwoAdvantageKOnePHolm$),
\rqTwoAdvantageKFiveDiff{} at five ($p = \rqTwoAdvantageKFivePHolm$) and level at ten
(\rqTwoAdvantageKTenDiff{} points, $p = \rqTwoAdvantageKTenPHolm$). Both results held
without the development patients, with the chronological draw and on the post-therapy
session ($p = \rqTwoHTwoAPostP$ and $\rqTwoHTwoBPostP$; the same with sub-21 and sub-22
averaged).

\textbf{Per patient.} \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSixtyBoth{} patients reached 60\,\% with both
methods; for them transfer needed a median of \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSixtyMedianSaved{}
fewer trials of each movement. \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSixtyOnlyScratch{} reached it only
from scratch, because transfer levelled off below it, \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSixtyOnlyTransfer{}
only with transfer, and \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSixtyNeither{} with neither. At 70\,\%,
\rqTwoReachRestAhSfSeventyBoth{} reached it with both, \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSeventyOnlyScratch{}
only from scratch, \rqTwoReachRestAhSfSeventyOnlyTransfer{} only with transfer and
\rqTwoReachRestAhSfSeventyNeither{} with neither.

\textbf{The network.} The pre-trained EEGNet was the strongest method at small budgets
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}): \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfEegnetEaFtKZeroMean} with no labelled trial
and \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfEegnetEaFtKFiveMean} with five, against
\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfEegnetScratchKFiveMean} for EEGNet trained from scratch. It too was
caught only with the whole pool (\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfEegnetScratchKAllMean} against
\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfEegnetEaFtKAllMean}). This pre-registered secondary pair supported
both hypotheses: the area under the learning curve was larger with pre-training (mean
difference \rqTwoEegnetHTwoADiff, CI \rqTwoEegnetHTwoADiffCiLo{} to
\rqTwoEegnetHTwoADiffCiHi, $W = \rqTwoEegnetHTwoAW$, $p = \rqTwoEegnetHTwoAP$), and its
advantage shrank with the whole pool by \rqTwoEegnetHTwoBPointsDiff{} points
($p = \rqTwoEegnetHTwoBP$). Its fine-tuning was set to the least adaptive candidate
values, and no claim rests on this pair alone. With five trials, the methods ranked in
this order: pre-trained EEGNet, re-centred TS+LR, CSP+LDA, pooled TS+LR, MDM, TS+LR,
re-centred MDM, EEGNet from scratch (Friedman test, $p = \rqTwoFriedmanRestAhSfKFiveP$).

\section{Can the Sixth Finger Be a Command of Its Own? (RQ3)}\label{sec:res-command}

\textbf{Answer: offline, as a candidate ``move now'' switch against rest; as a command
distinct from the hand, not yet.}

\subsection{The planned test}
In the planned scenario, the patient's hand trials were already recorded and $k$
sixth-finger trials were added. Outcome (a), detecting the new command against rest, uses
no hand trial in either model (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), so it measures how fast a
newly calibrated command is detected, not a command added to a hand model. Transfer
reached \pct{\rqThreeSfRestRctTslrKOneMean} with one sixth-finger trial against
\pct{\rqThreeSfRestTsLrKOneMean} from scratch. Its advantage in area under the curve was
not significant ($W = \rqThreeHThreeAW$, $p = \rqThreeHThreeAP$, Holm-adjusted
$\rqThreeHThreeAPHolm$, $r = \rqThreeHThreeAR$). Outcome (b), the three-class task, does
use the hand trials. There the model trained from scratch, on unbalanced classes without
class weights, almost never answered ``sixth finger'' (between
\pct{\rqThreeRestAhSfTsLrSfRecallMin} and \pct{\rqThreeRestAhSfTsLrSfRecallMax} of the
sixth-finger windows for up to five trials), while transfer did for
\pct{\rqThreeRestAhSfRctTslrKFiveSfRecall}; still, the advantage in area under the curve
was small ($p = \rqThreeHThreeBP$). \textbf{H3 is \rqThreeHThreeACategory} on this
session. It was supported on the post-therapy session (Holm-adjusted
$p = \rqThreeHThreeAPostPHolm$), so it did not replicate. The secondary pair, EEGNet
pre-trained and fine-tuned against EEGNet trained from scratch, favoured transfer on both
outcomes (sixth finger against rest: ALC difference \rqThreeEegnetHThreeADiff, CI
\rqThreeEegnetHThreeADiffCiLo{} to \rqThreeEegnetHThreeADiffCiHi, Holm-adjusted
$p = \rqThreeEegnetHThreeAPHolm$; three classes: \rqThreeEegnetHThreeBDiff,
$p = \rqThreeEegnetHThreeBPHolm$). EEGNet trained from scratch is weak at these budgets,
though (\pct{\rqThreeSfRestEegnetScratchKOneMean} on sixth finger against rest with one
trial), so this says more about the network than about the command.

Whether the sixth finger adds anything to a hand model is the question of the
pre-registered control, and it changed how the switch must be read. The patient's own
hand-versus-rest model, which never saw a sixth-finger trial and simply counts any imagined
movement as ``sixth finger'', detected the sixth-finger windows at
\pct{\rqThreeSfRestAhProxyMean}. That is better than the transfer model at two and at
five trials (\pct{\rqThreeSfRestRctTslrKTwoMean} and \pct{\rqThreeSfRestRctTslrKFiveMean}):
transfer minus the control was \rqThreeHThreeCKTwoPointsDiff{} and
\rqThreeHThreeCKFivePointsDiff{} points, with both confidence intervals below zero.
\textbf{H3c is \rqThreeHThreeCKTwoCategory}: the test was one-sided, so an effect in the
other direction cannot be called contrary, and the sixth-finger model added nothing over
a detector of any imagery. The models trained to
detect the sixth finger also called most \emph{hand} windows ``sixth finger'' (the
own-hand control \pct{\rqThreeHandAsFingerAhProxy}, transfer \pct{\rqThreeHandAsFingerRctTslrKFive}
at five trials). As the pre-registration foresaw, we therefore claim no distinct
sixth-finger command.

\subsection{A closer look, after the plan (post hoc)}
A follow-up analysis added after the planned results asked the switch question directly
(Figure~\ref{fig:switch}). Its settings were fixed before it was run, and TS+LR on the 14
central electrodes scored best among its candidates on runs 1 and 2, but this
configuration's test score was not new: the channel check of

TS+LR on the 14 central electrodes told sixth-finger imagery from rest
\pct{\exFourTsLrCentralMean} of the time (\ci{\exFourTsLrCentralCiLo}{\exFourTsLrCentralCiHi});

\section{What Did the Exploratory Analyses Add?}\label{sec:res-explore}

\textbf{Answer: a model needs a short top-up each day; played continuously, the
sixth-finger command caught only \pct{\rqFourReplayFingerDetectMin} to
\pct{\rqFourReplayFingerDetectMax} of the sixth-finger periods; and none of the later ideas
made hand and sixth finger separable.} Only the expectations of H4 were written down in
advance, without a test; everything else in this section was added after the results.

\textbf{Another day (RQ4).} A model built before therapy and used unchanged scored
\pct{\rqFourLaterPostUnchangedMean} two weeks later, alike after BCI training
(\pct{\rqFourLaterPostUnchangedGroupBMean}) and after conventional therapy
(\pct{\rqFourLaterPostUnchangedGroupTMean}), and \pct{\rqFourLaterFollowUnchangedMean} a
month after that, on three choices (Figure~\ref{fig:later}). Re-centring it on the new
day's rest, with no labels, recovered part of the loss
(\pct{\rqFourLaterPostRecentredKZeroMean} and \pct{\rqFourLaterFollowRecentredKZeroMean}).
Five new practice trials of each movement on the new day, combined with the old data,
brought it back to \pct{\rqFourLaterPostRecentredKFiveMean} and
\pct{\rqFourLaterFollowRecentredKFiveMean} when re-centred together, and to
\pct{\rqFourLaterPostPooledKFiveMean} and \pct{\rqFourLaterFollowPooledKFiveMean} when
simply pooled without re-centring: with new trials, re-centring added nothing, and it
helped only when no new trial was recorded. Both come close to the
\pct{\rqFourLaterPostWithinPostMean} of a model built entirely on the new day.

\textbf{Swapping sides (RQ4).} Mirroring the electrodes of patients with a weak left hand,
so that every patient's affected side lines up, did not help transfer: it changed the
three-class score by \rqFourMirrorRctTslrDiffMin{} to \rqFourMirrorRctTslrDiffMax{} points
for re-centred TS+LR, and no difference was significant (smallest Holm-adjusted $p =
\rqFourMirrorPHolmMin$; Holm across budgets, as planned, although the analysis is
exploratory). This fits the weakening on both sides of the head seen in
Section~\ref{sec:res-erd}.

\textbf{Running continuously (RQ4, pre-registered expectation).} We replayed each
patient's test runs as one unbroken signal, read every half second, through the
demonstrator's command gate, with no cue telling the model when to look, as defined
before any replay. H4 expected the sixth-finger command to be detected in most
sixth-finger periods. The three-class models trained on the whole practice pool (CSP+LDA,
TS+LR, re-centred TS+LR and EEGNet from scratch) issued it in only
\pct{\rqFourReplayFingerDetectMin} to \pct{\rqFourReplayFingerDetectMax} of the
sixth-finger periods, with \rqFourReplayFalsePerMinMin{} to \rqFourReplayFalsePerMinMax{}
false activations per minute of rest and an information transfer rate of
\rqFourReplayItrMin{} to \rqFourReplayItrMax{} bits per minute, so the expectation of H4
did not hold. Read afterwards as one switch for any imagined movement (post hoc), they
caught \pct{\rqFourSwitchDetectMin} to \pct{\rqFourSwitchDetectMax} of the imagery periods,
with \rqFourSwitchFalsePerMinMin{} to \rqFourSwitchFalsePerMinMax{} false switch-ons per
minute of rest, about \rqFourSwitchLatencyMin{} to \rqFourSwitchLatencyMax{} seconds after
the cue. That is better, but false switch-ons at rest are still too frequent for a device,
and none of this was tested with a patient in the loop.

\textbf{Is it the picture on the screen? (post hoc)} Rest is recorded while a cross is
shown and imagery while a picture is shown, so a model might read the screen rather than
the imagined movement. With only the 14 central electrodes, sixth finger against rest
reached \pct{\rqFourCueSfRestCentralMean}; with only the five electrodes over the visual
cortex, \pct{\rqFourCueSfRestParietoOccipitalMean}. The switch is carried mainly by the
motor area, though a visual part cannot be ruled out. For hand against sixth finger the
visual electrodes did better: \pct{\rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalMean}
(\ci{\rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalCiLo}{\rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalCiHi}) against
\pct{\rqFourCueAhSfCentralMean}
(\ci{\rqFourCueAhSfCentralCiLo}{\rqFourCueAhSfCentralCiHi}), a difference of
\rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalVsCentralDiff{} points
(\cipts{\rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalVsCentralDiffCiLo}{\rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalVsCentralDiffCiHi},
$p = \rqFourCueAhSfParietoOccipitalVsCentralP$). Part of that weak signal may therefore be
the two pictures rather than the two movements. After the common average reference, a
comparison of electrode groups cannot fully separate motor from visual sources.

\section{Does the Demonstrator Work?}\label{sec:res-demo}

\textbf{Answer: yes.} The trained model runs live on a real patient's held-out recording,
on a server or in any browser, and its decisions move the drawn hand.

The demonstrator's model is EEGNet, the calibration study's best method with the whole
practice pool (\pct{\demoChoiceEegnetScratchMean} on three choices), trained on runs 1 and 2
of patient \demoSubject. The protocol picks the patient whose score lies closest to the
group's median (\pct{\demoCohortMedianAcc}), so that the demonstration is typical rather
than the best case. With an even number of patients the median falls halfway between the
two middle ones, \demoSubject{} (\pct{\demoChosenByAcc}) and \demoTieSubject{}
(\pct{\demoTieAcc}); the tie was broken in favour of the one with more whole held-out
trials. On the
\demoTrials{} whole held-out trials it carries, the model scored \pct{\demoHeldOutAcc} on

\demoMoveRestN{} windows (\pct{\demoMoveRestAcc}); hand from sixth finger, in
\demoHandFingerRight{} of \demoHandFingerN{} (\pct{\demoHandFingerAcc}). These are one
patient's numbers and are higher than the group's; the group results above are the ones
to quote.

The exported model gives the same answers as the original: the largest difference was

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the published number was high}
CSP is a supervised step: it picks the filters that best separate the labelled trials it
is given. With 40 electrodes and a few dozen trials per class, there is enough freedom to
separate even noise, so when the test trials are among those it is given, it separates
them too~\cite{kapoor2023,varoquaux2017}. The pattern in Table~\ref{tab:rqone} is the
signature of this: the more an early step learned from labels, the larger the gap, and
steps that never saw a label showed none. This is a common, subtle pitfall, not a sign of
bad faith. The authors released their data and code, and that openness is what made this
check possible. We did not reproduce the published value itself: our rebuild of the
authors' recipe scored higher with the leak. Leakage-free cross-validation can fall below
chance when there is little signal, so we also gave the run-wise splits, and the
inflation held against all three. For anyone using the dataset, the lesson is simple: fit
every step that sees labels inside the training data, split by run or session, and report
the leakage-free score next to any comparison with the published one.

\subsection{What calibration really costs}
Transfer, as configured here, bought time. A new patient got from the other patients
what ten trials of their own would give, after one trial of each movement. But the
transfer model then hardly moved, and with the whole practice pool the patient's own model
ended numerically higher, a difference we did not test. These curves describe one
configuration, not transfer in general. With a target weight of one, each of the
newcomer's windows counted as much as one of the other patients' thousands, a weight
chosen from candidates that all scored near guessing, and the pre-trained network was
likewise set to its least adaptive values. Our reading, which we did not test, is that
the model therefore stays mostly theirs. A weight that grows as practice accumulates, a
switch to the patient's own model chosen by cross-validation within the practice data,
or Riemannian Procrustes analysis~\cite{rodrigues2019} are the obvious next designs. Part
of the early lead also reflects how little a model trained from scratch learns from four
windows: with no labelled trial at all, transfer already matched it at five trials of
each movement. Stroke makes transfer hard in any case: lesions differ in place and size,
and EEG responses vary with lesion location~\cite{park2016}. The day-to-day results add a
practical point: calibration is not a one-off. A short top-up at the start of each
session, pooled with or re-centred on the earlier session, is part of the cost of using
such a device. These findings agree with transfer studies in healthy
people~\cite{he2020,zanini2018,wu2022review} and with studies in stroke patients that
reused a patient's own earlier sessions~\cite{giles2022,cao2021}, which also report the
largest gains when the new data are scarce or the session starts with low accuracy.

\subsection{What the sixth finger can be}
The data support a switch, not a second command. Imagery against rest was read well, and
the patient's own hand model detected sixth-finger imagery as well as any sixth-finger
model did. Telling the two imagery types apart stayed between \pct{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeMean} and
\pct{\exFourAhSfEegnetEaFtMean} under every leakage-free test, the upper value an
optimistic best of many, and the follow-ups did not change that; part of that weak signal
may even be the two pictures on the screen. Two observations suggest this is not only a
weakness of our models. The rhythm fell almost equally for both imagery types (an
observation made before the pre-registration, which declared that it would not be tested
again), and healthy people could not tell two grasps of one hand apart across days
either. For a wearable sixth finger, a reliable ``move now'' switch may be enough: the
finger has one job, to close the grip. The switch found here is only a candidate. Played
continuously it caught \pct{\exFiveFingerFingerOnMean} of the sixth-finger periods, with
\exFiveFingerFalsePerMinMean{} false switch-ons per minute of rest and about
\exFiveFingerLatencyMean{} seconds into a 6-second period, it also switched on for hand
imagery, and it was never tested with a patient in the loop or used to drive a finger.
A closed-loop study must show whether a patient can use it.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One centre, few patients.} The \nSubjects{} patients came from one hospital
  (\nPre{} in the main tests), so the results may not hold for other sites, devices or kinds
  of stroke. Per-patient results are shown for that reason.
  \item \textbf{Two therapies.} Between sessions, \nPostGroupB{} patients had BCI training
  with the sixth finger and \nPostGroupT{} conventional therapy; the later-day results were
  alike in both groups.
  \item \textbf{Offline only.} Calibration was simulated on recorded runs, with no feedback
  to the patient. The continuous replay is closer to real use but is not a closed-loop
  test, and the robotic finger was worn but never driven by a model.
  \item \textbf{Weak baselines at small budgets.} A model trained from scratch has four
  windows at one trial of each movement, and in the new-command scenario its classes are
  unbalanced and carry no class weights, as pre-registered. A regularised or
  class-weighted baseline may narrow transfer's early lead.
  \item \textbf{Someone else's cleaning.} The authors cleaned the data partly by hand,
  possibly using all trials of a session, and this cannot be repeated exactly.
  \item \textbf{The screen may help.} Rest and imagery were recorded in front of
  different pictures, and the robotic finger was worn during sixth-finger imagery. The
  cue check reduces this concern for the switch but does not remove it, and the weak
  hand-against-finger signal was at least as strong over the visual cortex.
  \item \textbf{Two development patients and the sources.} The transfer settings were
  chosen on sub-01 and sub-03, who are also among the tested patients; the results without
  them agree. The other patients' windows used in that choice included their test runs.
  \item \textbf{A git-dated plan.} The pre-registration was frozen in a git tag, whose
  dates are git's own, not with an independent registry.
  \item \textbf{The published number is not reproduced exactly.} The replica overshoots it;
  we reproduce the direction of the leak, not the value.
  \item \textbf{Exploratory parts.} The follow-ups were added after the planned results;
  their many comparisons are uncorrected, and they guide future work rather than prove
  anything.
  \item \textbf{No hardware.} The demonstrator replays recorded EEG and moves a drawn hand.
\end{itemize}

\section{Chapter Summary}
The published protocol inflates accuracy through a leak; evaluated without leakage, hand and sixth finger
are only weakly separable, while imagery against rest is read well. With the one
configuration we tested, learning from other patients gave a new patient a fast start
almost at once and then stayed nearly flat; with the whole practice pool a patient's own
model ended numerically, not significantly, higher. Offline, the sixth finger can be read
as a candidate ``move now'' switch against rest, not as a command distinct from the hand,
and played continuously that switch caught fewer than half of the sixth-finger periods.
The demonstrator shows the trained model at work on a real patient's recording. The last
chapter draws these threads together.
