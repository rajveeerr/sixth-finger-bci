This chapter reviews the work our project builds on. It follows the questions a reader
would ask, in order: how a brain--computer interface reads imagined movement
(Section~\ref{sec:lit-mi}), how the signals are decoded (Section~\ref{sec:lit-decoding}), how

and the chapter ends with the gaps this project addresses.

\section{Motor-Imagery Brain--Computer Interfaces}\label{sec:lit-mi}

A \emph{brain--computer interface} (BCI) is a system that turns measured brain activity into
a command for a machine. Wolpaw and colleagues defined it as a channel of communication
that does not depend on the brain's normal outputs, the nerves and muscles of the
body~\cite{wolpaw2002}. Early BCIs were built for people who are almost completely
paralysed, for whom any reliable channel matters~\cite{kubler2001}.

Most non-invasive BCIs use the \emph{electroencephalogram} (EEG). EEG is the record of tiny
voltages picked up by electrodes placed on the scalp. It is safe, cheap and portable, which
is why it can be used outside a laboratory. It is also weak and noisy: the skull blurs the
signal, and the muscles and the eyes add voltages of their own.

In a \emph{motor-imagery} (MI) BCI the user imagines a movement without making it. The
motor cortex, the strip of the brain that plans and controls movement, produces rhythmic
activity when it is idle: the mu rhythm, at about 8 to 13 cycles per second, and the beta
rhythm, at about 13 to 30. When a person moves or imagines moving, these rhythms weaken over
the part of the cortex that controls the body part. This drop in power is called
\emph{event-related desynchronisation} (ERD). When the movement ends, the rhythm often comes
back stronger than before, which is called \emph{event-related synchronisation}
(ERS)~\cite{pfurtscheller1999}.

Imagined movements of different body parts can be told apart because the motor cortex is
laid out like a map of the body (Figure~\ref{fig:homunculus}). The hand, the foot and the
tongue each have their own region, and each half of the brain controls the opposite side of
the body. Imagining the right hand therefore weakens the rhythm over the left half of the
brain more than over the right. Pfurtscheller and colleagues showed that such differences
can be classified from single trials and used as commands
\cite{pfurtscheller2001,pfurtscheller2006}.

Motor imagery does not work equally well for everyone. A sizeable share of users never
reach reliable control with standard methods, a problem called ``BCI illiteracy'' or, more
neutrally, BCI inefficiency~\cite{vidaurre2010,ahn2015}. A large study of healthy volunteers
using three kinds of BCI found the same wide spread between people~\cite{lee2019openbmi}.
This matters for our project: an average accuracy can hide users for whom a system does not
work at all, so results must also be shown person by person.

\section{Decoding Methods}\label{sec:lit-decoding}

A \emph{decoder} is the part of a BCI that looks at a short piece of EEG and decides which
movement was imagined. It is learned from labelled examples, that is, pieces of EEG for
which the imagined movement is known. Three families of decoders are common, and our
project compares all three.

\subsection{Spatial filters with a linear classifier}

\emph{Common spatial patterns} (CSP) is the classic method for motor imagery. It learns a
few weighted sums of the electrodes, called spatial filters, chosen so that the filtered
signal has high power during one class and low power during the other. The logarithm of
that power is a compact feature of each trial~\cite{ramoser2000,blankertz2008}.
\emph{Filter-bank CSP} repeats this in several frequency bands and keeps the most useful
features~\cite{ang2008fbcsp}.

The features then go to a simple classifier. \emph{Linear discriminant analysis} (LDA)
draws the straight boundary that best separates the classes when each class is spread like
a bell curve. A \emph{support vector machine} (SVM) instead places the boundary as far as
possible from the nearest examples of each class~\cite{cortes1995}. With few trials, the
averages and spreads that CSP and LDA estimate become unstable. \emph{Shrinkage} pulls these
estimates towards a simpler, steadier form and removes much of the problem~\cite{ledoit2004}.

One property of CSP is central to Chapter~\ref{ch:results}: CSP is \emph{supervised}. It
uses the class labels to choose its filters. If test trials are allowed into that choice,
the filters are tuned to separate exactly those trials, and the test score rises for a
reason that has nothing to do with the brain.

\subsection{Riemannian methods}

A second family describes each trial by its \emph{covariance matrix}: a table that holds the
power at every electrode and how strongly every pair of electrodes varies together. These
matrices live on a curved surface, studied in \emph{Riemannian geometry}, so ordinary
straight-line distances between them are misleading. Barachant and colleagues measured
distances along the curve instead~\cite{barachant2012}. Their \emph{minimum distance to mean}
(MDM) classifier gives a new trial the class whose average matrix is nearest. Their
\emph{tangent-space} method flattens the matrices around a central point, so that any
standard classifier, such as logistic regression, can be used on them. Reviews describe
these methods as simple, robust and well suited to small amounts of data
\cite{congedo2017,yger2017}.

\subsection{Deep learning}

Neural networks can learn their own filters straight from the EEG. \emph{EEGNet} is a small
convolutional network designed for EEG~\cite{lawhern2018}. It first learns filters in time,
which act like frequency bands, then learns a spatial filter for each of them, much as CSP
does, and finally combines the results. Lawhern and colleagues found that it matched the
best traditional method on several kinds of BCI data while learning far fewer weights than
larger networks. Schirrmeister and colleagues introduced shallow and deep convolutional
networks for EEG~\cite{schirrmeister2017}, and their code grew into the braindecode toolbox
that we use~\cite{braindecode}. Newer networks add attention layers~\cite{song2023conformer},
and very large models are now pre-trained on thousands of hours of EEG from many people
\cite{kostas2021bendr,jiang2024labram,wang2025cbramod}.

Two cautions come with deep learning. A systematic review found that many deep-learning EEG
studies are hard to reproduce~\cite{roy2019}. And a ten-year review of BCI classifiers
concluded that, when training data are scarce, shrinkage LDA and Riemannian classifiers
remain among the most reliable choices~\cite{lotte2018review}. We therefore treat EEGNet as
one candidate among several, not as the expected winner.

Finally, the weights of a linear decoder do not show directly where its useful signal lies.
Haufe and colleagues showed how to turn weights into \emph{patterns} that can be read as maps
of the brain~\cite{haufe2014}; we use them to check what our decoders rely on.

\section{Calibration and Transfer Learning}\label{sec:lit-transfer}

Before a new user can control an MI-BCI, they must record labelled trials from which the
decoder learns their pattern. This step is called \emph{calibration}. It takes time and
effort, which is a real barrier for tired or impaired users, and it is usually repeated in
each session because the EEG changes from day to day. Lotte reviewed ways to shorten or
remove it~\cite{lotte2015}.

\emph{Transfer learning} is the idea behind most of these ways: knowledge learned from other
people, or from earlier sessions, is reused for the new user. Early work built decoders from
many other users~\cite{fazli2009} or from the same user's earlier
sessions~\cite{krauledat2008}, so that little or no new training was needed, and adapted
the classifier while it was in use~\cite{shenoy2006}. Jayaram and colleagues framed transfer
between BCI users as learning several related tasks together~\cite{jayaram2016}.

The difficulty is that every person's EEG sits in a different place. Head size, electrode
position and the anatomy of the brain shift the signals, so a decoder trained on other
people often fails on a new one. Two \emph{alignment} methods address this:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Riemannian re-centring} moves each person's covariance matrices so that their
        average becomes the same reference matrix, the identity. After this step the data of
        different people overlap and can be pooled~\cite{zanini2018}. Later work also matched
        the spread and the rotation of the data~\cite{rodrigues2019}, and a related method for
        another kind of BCI combined the class averages of other users with those of the new
        user~\cite{kalunga2018}.
  \item \emph{Euclidean alignment} applies the same idea to the raw trials. It whitens each
        person's trials with the inverse square root of their average covariance, so that any
        decoder, including a neural network, can be trained on the aligned
        data~\cite{he2020}. It needs no labels from the new user. Its authors note that
        outlying trials can distort the reference and that their online tests were
        simulated.
\end{itemize}

Wu and colleagues reviewed transfer learning for EEG BCIs and found alignment and
fine-tuning to be the most common tools~\cite{wu2022review}. \emph{Fine-tuning} means taking a
network trained on other people and training it a little further on the new user's few
trials. Ding and colleagues recently used this approach to move a robotic hand finger by
finger from EEG~\cite{ding2025}, and continuous control of a robotic arm from non-invasive
signals has also been shown~\cite{edelman2019}. All of this work used commands that people
already know, such as moving their own hand or fingers.

\section{Supernumerary Limbs and Sixth-Finger Motor Imagery}\label{sec:lit-sixth}

A \emph{supernumerary} robotic limb is an extra robotic body part worn on the body, such as a
third arm or an extra finger. It adds to the body rather than replacing a missing part.
Reviews describe many designs, from wearable fingers that help one hand to grip, to extra
arms for industrial work~\cite{prattichizzo2021,tong2021}. Eden and colleagues set out the
principles and the open problems of such \emph{movement augmentation}, and named the control
signal as one of the hardest~\cite{eden2022}.

There is good evidence that the brain can learn to control an extra digit. People born with
a sixth finger on each hand move it independently and can do with one hand some tasks that
need two hands for other people~\cite{mehring2019}. Healthy people who wore a robotic ``Third
Thumb'' for a few days learned to use it, and imaging showed that the brain's map of their
hand changed~\cite{kieliba2021}. A third robotic arm has been driven by a brain--machine
interface while the user did a second task with both natural arms~\cite{penaloza2018}.
Outside such BCI studies, however, extra digits are usually driven by another body part,
for example by pressure from the toes.

A research group at Tianjin University introduced \emph{sixth-finger motor imagery}: the
user imagines moving a robotic sixth finger worn on the hand. In healthy volunteers,
imagining the sixth finger produced stronger ERD and better classification than imagining a
natural limb~\cite{wang2024jbhi}. The same group reported stronger ERD and stronger
connections between brain regions when stroke patients imagined the sixth
finger~\cite{wang2024tnsre}. Related studies described the EEG of sixth-finger imagery and
chose the electrodes most useful for classifying it~\cite{liu2022jne}, and a small pilot
found changes in resting brain activity after several weeks of BCI training with a
supernumerary finger~\cite{liu2022frontiers}.

These studies make the sixth finger a promising command. They do not ask how many trials a
decoder needs to learn it, or whether it can still be told apart from imagining the
patient's own weak hand once the evaluation is strict.

\section{Brain--Computer Interfaces in Stroke Rehabilitation}\label{sec:lit-stroke}

A stroke often leaves one arm and hand weak. BCIs are studied as a way to help recovery: the
patient tries or imagines a movement, the BCI detects it and a device responds, which closes
the loop between the intention and the movement. A meta-analysis of controlled trials found
that BCI-based therapy improved arm function after stroke more than the control
treatments~\cite{cervera2018}. In one trial, stimulation of the arm muscles triggered by the
brain produced recovery that lasted after the therapy had ended~\cite{biasiucci2018}. Other
randomised trials combined motor imagery with a robot~\cite{ang2015rct} or with a picture of
the patient's own hand as feedback~\cite{pichiorri2015}.

Stroke also makes decoding harder. The damaged area differs between patients, and the EEG
response to movement changes with the location of the lesion~\cite{park2016}. A pattern
learned from one patient may therefore not fit the next, which is the core difficulty for
transfer learning in this group.

Several public EEG datasets of stroke patients have appeared recently: for acute
stroke~\cite{liu2024acute}, for chronic stroke~\cite{lu2026chronic} and for imagined leg
movements~\cite{liu2025lowerlimb}. The dataset we use was released by Wang and colleagues in
2026~\cite{wang2026dataset}. It holds the EEG of \nSubjects{} stroke patients from one
hospital who imagined either grasping with the affected hand or moving a robotic sixth
finger, recorded before two weeks of therapy, right after it and one month later.
\nPostGroupB{} patients received BCI training with the robotic finger and \nPostGroupT{}
received conventional therapy. To our knowledge it is the only public dataset of
sixth-finger imagery in stroke patients.

The dataset paper reports that CSP followed by LDA separated hand imagery from sixth-finger
imagery with an accuracy of \pct{\rqOnePublishedGroupBCspLda} in the BCI group and
\pct{\rqOnePublishedGroupTCspLda} in the conventional group before therapy, and between
\pct{\rqOnePublishedFollowGroupTCspSvm} and \pct{\rqOnePublishedFollowGroupBCspSvm} across all
groups, sessions and classifiers. The features were the power of the mu rhythm over time,
relative to a baseline before the cue, a measure introduced by Makeig~\cite{makeig1993}. The
paper does not say at which stage the CSP filters were fitted. The analysis script released
with it fits them on all the trials of a session before the data are split for testing.
Section~\ref{sec:lit-pitfalls} explains why this matters, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results}
measures its effect.

\section{Evaluation Pitfalls}\label{sec:lit-pitfalls}

A decoder's accuracy means something only if it was measured on trials the decoder never
learned from. The usual tool is \emph{cross-validation}: the trials are split into several
parts, called folds; the decoder learns from all folds but one and is tested on the one left
out, and this is repeated until every fold has been tested once. Several ways of getting
this wrong are known.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Leakage.} \emph{Leakage} is any route by which information from the test data
        reaches the training. Kapoor and Narayanan found it across many fields that use
        machine learning, where it produced results that others could not
        reproduce~\cite{kapoor2023}. Every step that learns from data, including spatial
        filters, feature selection and scaling, must be fitted inside the training folds
        only~\cite{varoquaux2017,lemm2011}.
  \item \textbf{Splitting at the wrong level.} If short segments of one recording are spread
        over the training and the test folds, the decoder can recognise the recording, or the
        person, rather than the brain state. Brookshire and colleagues showed that splitting
        clinical EEG by segment instead of by person greatly inflated the accuracy of deep
        networks~\cite{brookshire2024}. Splits by run, session or person avoid this.
  \item \textbf{Block designs.} When all trials of one class are recorded together, slow
        drifts in the signal differ between the classes, and a decoder can learn the drift
        instead of the brain state~\cite{li2021perils}.
  \item \textbf{Misleading chance levels.} With two classes, guessing is right half the time
        only on average. On a small test set a decoder can beat that by luck alone, so the
        threshold for ``better than chance'' must come from the binomial distribution for the
        actual number of test trials~\cite{combrisson2015}.
  \item \textbf{Many comparisons.} Comparing many decoders over many people calls for tests
        based on ranks, and for a correction when several tests are run, so that one lucky
        result is not over-read~\cite{demsar2006}.
\end{itemize}

Shared benchmarks help to avoid these traps. The MOABB framework runs many decoders on many
public datasets under the same fixed evaluation~\cite{jayaram2018moabb,chevallier2024moabb}.
Two of the public datasets it includes, dataset 2a of BCI Competition IV~\cite{tangermann2012}
and the dataset of Cho and colleagues~\cite{cho2017}, are the ones we use to check whether
the leakage found in the stroke data also appears in healthy volunteers.

Three points stand out. The methods for decoding and for shortening calibration were built
almost entirely on healthy people imagining familiar movements. The only public
sixth-finger dataset reports high accuracy, but with spatial filters fitted before the data
were split. And the evaluation literature names exactly that step as a common cause of
inflated results.

\section{Research Gap}\label{sec:lit-gap}

The review leaves three questions open.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An honest benchmark of the dataset.} Although the dataset paper reports that
        hand and sixth-finger imagery can be told apart with high accuracy, it remains unknown
        how well this works when every fitted step stays inside the training data, and
        whether the same evaluation step inflates results on other datasets too.
  \item \textbf{Calibration for a new command in stroke patients.} Although transfer learning
        is known to shorten calibration for familiar movements in healthy users, it remains
        unknown how much practice a new stroke patient needs before a decoder recognises the
        sixth finger, and whether learning from other patients reduces it.
  \item \textbf{A command of its own.} Although sixth-finger imagery produces a clear change in
        the EEG, it remains unknown whether a decoder can treat it as a command separate from
        imagining the affected hand, or only as a sign that some movement is being imagined.
\end{enumerate}

The data and the protocol we used are in Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:protocol}; the
answers are in Sections~\ref{sec:res-leak}, \ref{sec:res-calib} and~\ref{sec:res-command}.

\section{Chapter Summary}\label{sec:lit-end}

In short, spatial filters, Riemannian methods and compact networks all decode imagined
movement well in healthy people, and alignment and fine-tuning cut the practice a new user
must give. For the sixth finger after stroke, three things are missing: an evaluation that
keeps test data out of every fitted step, a measure of the calibration the new command
needs, and a test of whether it is a command of its own. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} sets out how
we answered them.
