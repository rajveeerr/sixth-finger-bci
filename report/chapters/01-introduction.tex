\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}
% Sources: report-material 01 and 03 (project-report-guide.md §2), rewritten.

This chapter explains why the project exists and what it set out to answer. It starts
with the medical problem, a weak hand after stroke, and the idea of a robotic sixth finger
that helps that hand grip. It then asks whether brain signals can move such a finger, and
why that question needs a careful test. It ends with the research questions, the limits of
the work, what is new in it, and a map of the report.

\section{Background}\label{sec:intro-background}

\subsection{A weak hand after stroke}
A stroke cuts the blood supply to part of the brain. When the damaged part controls
movement, one side of the body is often left weak. The hand is a common and lasting
problem: many people regain some movement of the arm but still cannot open and close
the hand well enough to hold a cup or a bottle. Tasks that need two hands then become
hard or impossible.

Rehabilitation tries to restore movement through practice. Some newer methods add
technology to that practice. One of them reads the brain's own signals, so that the
patient's intention to move can trigger help even when the hand itself barely
moves~\cite{cervera2018,biasiucci2018,pichiorri2015}.

\subsection{Reading movement intention from the scalp}
The \emph{electroencephalogram} (EEG) is the very small voltage that brain cells produce
together, measured by electrodes placed on the scalp. The electrodes sit in a cap
(Figure~\ref{fig:bciphoto}). EEG is safe, portable and cheap compared with other ways of
measuring the brain. Its drawbacks are that the signal is weak, noisy and different from
one person to the next, and even from one day to the next for the same person.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{external/bci-experiment-krol.jpg}
  \caption[An EEG experiment]{An EEG experiment. The person wears a cap of electrodes; a computer records
  the brain's signals while a screen shows what to do. Image: Laurens R.\ Krol, Wikimedia
  Commons, CC BY 4.0.}
  \label{fig:bciphoto}
\end{figure}

A \emph{brain--computer interface} (BCI) turns measured brain activity
into a command for a device, without using the nerves and muscles that normally carry
the command~\cite{wolpaw2002}. Figure~\ref{fig:bciloop} shows the loop: the signal is
recorded, the computer picks out its useful parts (the \emph{features}) and a
\emph{classifier} decides what the person intended. The command acts on the world, and
the person sees the result.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{external/bci-diagram-krol.png}
  \caption[The loop of every BCI]{The loop of every BCI. Follow the arrows: the brain signal is measured, its
  features are extracted and classified, the command acts on a device, and the user sees
  the outcome and adjusts. Image: Laurens R.\ Krol, Wikimedia Commons, CC0.}
  \label{fig:bciloop}
\end{figure}

This project uses \emph{motor imagery} (MI): the person imagines a movement without
making it. Imagining a movement changes the EEG over the motor cortex, the strip of the
brain that controls movement. At rest, the cells there fire in step and produce a
rhythm of about 8 to 13 cycles per second, called the mu rhythm. When the person imagines
moving, the cells fall out of step and the rhythm becomes weaker
(Figure~\ref{fig:rhythm}). This weakening is called \emph{event-related
desynchronisation} (ERD)~\cite{pfurtscheller1999,pfurtscheller2001}. A computer model
trained on labelled examples can learn to spot it and name the imagined movement.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{web/rhythm.pdf}
  \caption[What the model looks for]{What the model looks for. The black line is the rhythm over the hand area of
  the motor cortex while the patient imagines a movement; the grey line behind it is its
  size at rest. The wave is a drawing, but the drop in its size is the measured average
  over the patients of this study (Section~\ref{sec:res-erd}).}
  \label{fig:rhythm}
\end{figure}

\section{The Sixth Finger and Human Augmentation}\label{sec:intro-sixth}

A \emph{supernumerary} robotic finger is an extra, motor-driven finger worn on the body.
For a stroke patient it is strapped to the wrist of the weak hand, on the side of the
little finger (Figure~\ref{fig:sixth}). When it bends toward the palm, it presses an
object against the patient's own fingers and thumb, so the weak hand can hold things it
could not hold alone~\cite{prattichizzo2021,tong2021}.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/sixth-finger}
  \caption[The robotic sixth finger worn on the affected wrist]{The robotic sixth finger worn on the affected wrist (schematic drawn for this
  report). It bends toward the palm and closes the grip together with the patient's own
  fingers.}
  \label{fig:sixth}
\end{figure}

The finger must move when the wearer wants it to. A button is not always possible: the
hand that would press it may be the weak one. Reading the wish to move from the brain is
one answer. Wang and colleagues had healthy people~\cite{wang2024jbhi} and then stroke
patients~\cite{wang2024tnsre} imagine moving such a finger. They reported that this
\emph{sixth-finger imagery} produced a clear ERD, in some respects stronger than imagining a
movement of one's own hand.
Healthy people can also learn to use an extra robotic thumb, and the brain's map of the
hand changes as they do~\cite{kieliba2021,eden2022}.

Figure~\ref{fig:system} shows the whole chain that this project studies, from the cap to
the finger. Parts 1 and 2 record the brain signal. Part 3 is the piece of signal, four
seconds long, that the model reads. Part 4, the model, decides between rest, the hand and
the sixth finger, and part 5 is the finger that the decision would move.

  of signal the model reads, (4) the model, with one lamp for each answer, (5) the robotic
  finger. This project built and tested the part from the recorded signal to the model's
  decision; the finger is shown on screen as a drawing (Section~\ref{sec:limb}).}
  \label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

\section{Motivation and Problem Statement}\label{sec:intro-motivation}

\subsection{Every patient needs practice first}
A model cannot read a new patient's EEG out of the box. Brains, caps and strokes all
differ, so the patient must first perform a set of practice trials, each labelled with
the movement that was asked for. The model learns from these trials. This step is called
\emph{calibration}, and it costs the patient time and effort before the device does
anything useful~\cite{lotte2015}. The cost is likely higher for a sixth finger than for a
real hand, because the brain has never controlled such a finger and has no ready-made
pattern for it.

One way to shorten calibration is \emph{transfer learning}: the model first learns from
other people's recordings and then adapts to the new person with only a few
trials~\cite{he2020,zanini2018,wu2022review}. Whether this works for a new, artificial
command in stroke patients had not been measured.

\subsection{A public recording makes the question testable}
In 2026 Wang et al.\ published the EEG of \nSubjects{} stroke patients who imagined either
moving the affected hand or bending the sixth finger, recorded before and after two weeks
of therapy and again a month later~\cite{wang2026dataset}. Their data and code are open,
which let us study the question without recording new patients.

\subsection{A published number that looked too good}
The dataset paper reports that a standard method told the two kinds of imagery apart
with an accuracy of \pct{\rqOnePublishedGroupBCspLda} and \pct{\rqOnePublishedGroupTCspLda}
in its two patient groups before therapy~\cite{wang2026dataset}; guessing would score
50\,\%. When we read the authors' code, we found that its first step, a filter that
learns from the labels, was fitted on all trials, including the ones later used to test
it. A model tested on trials that shaped it is not tested on new data. This mistake is called
\emph{data leakage}, and it is a known cause of results that later fail to
hold~\cite{kapoor2023,brookshire2024}. Before any calibration study could be trusted, we
had to know what this data supports when every step learns from training trials only.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\begin{quote}
\emph{Using a public EEG recording of stroke patients, find out how well imagined movements
of the affected hand and of a robotic sixth finger can be told apart without leakage;
measure how much practice a new patient must give a model before it is useful, with and
without learning from other patients; and show the result working in a demonstrator that
anyone can open.}
\end{quote}

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}\label{sec:intro-rq}

The study was planned around four research questions (RQs). The hypotheses for the
first three were written down and frozen before the main experiments, a practice called
\emph{pre-registration} (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}).

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.1cm,style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1] \textbf{Is the published accuracy right?} What accuracy does a leakage-free test give
  for telling affected-hand imagery from sixth-finger imagery, compared with the published
  value? As a check, does the same mistake raise accuracy on two public recordings of
  healthy people (RQ1b)?
  \item[RQ2] \textbf{How much practice does a new patient need?} How does accuracy grow
  with the number of practice trials, and does learning from other patients reduce the
  number needed?
  \item[RQ3] \textbf{Can the sixth finger be added as a command of its own?} If a patient
  already has a working hand model, how many sixth-finger trials does it take to add the
  new command?
  \item[RQ4] \textbf{Exploratory questions}, planned but not pre-registered: does swapping
  the left and right sides of the head for left-side strokes help; does a model still work
  on another day; and how would it behave if it ran continuously, with no cue telling it
  when the patient starts?
\end{description}

A separate, pre-registered side study (RQ5) checked whether a related form of leakage
inflates results in another field, depression detection from EEG. It is reported briefly
in Section~\ref{sec:res-leak}.

The objectives that follow from these questions were to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item build a reproducible pipeline from the public files to labelled pieces of EEG,
  with an audit of the data's integrity;
  \item build an evaluation framework that cannot leak test data, with automatic checks
  before every model is fitted;
  \item test the published method without leakage and measure the size of the leak (RQ1);
  \item measure calibration curves for a new patient and a new command, with and without
  transfer (RQ2, RQ3);
  \item run the planned exploratory analyses (RQ4);
  \item build and deploy a demonstrator in which the decoded intention moves a drawn hand with
  a robotic sixth finger;
  \item document the work in this report and in a research paper.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope}\label{sec:intro-scope}

The project analyses data that were recorded by others and released publicly. It records
no new patients and builds no hardware. Its limits should be clear from the start:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It makes no medical claim. Nothing here shows that the method helps a patient
  recover.
  \item It does not read thoughts. A model recognises one of a few trained patterns.
  \item It does not use live EEG. The demonstrator replays a real patient's recording at
  its original speed.
  \item It moves no physical finger. The hand and the sixth finger are a drawing driven
  by the model's command.
  \item Calibration is simulated offline: the first runs of a recorded session serve as
  practice and the last runs as the test.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions and Novelty}\label{sec:intro-contrib}

The project makes four contributions. Their evidence is in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An honest benchmark of the sixth-finger dataset.} An integrity audit found
  recordings that appear twice in the release. Our rebuild of the authors' own recipe
  told hand from sixth finger \pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaPaperReplicaMean} of the time the
  published way (the dataset paper reports 85--86\,\%) and
  \pct{\rqOneCspLdaReplicaCvLeakfreeMean} without leakage; a standard pipeline scored
  \pct{\rqOneCspLdaPaperReplicaMean} and \pct{\rqOneCspLdaCvLeakfreeMean}, where guessing
  gives 50\,\%. The same
  mistake also inflated two public datasets of healthy people, by \rqOneBnciHOneBDiff{}
  and \rqOneChoHOneBDiff{} percentage points.
  \item \textbf{A pre-registered calibration study, the main research contribution.} For a
  new patient, a model that first learned from the other patients (in one configuration,
  which weighs each of the patient's windows like one of the others' thousands) reached
  \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKOneMean} on three choices (rest, hand, sixth finger) after one
  practice trial of each movement, about \rqTwoMinutesKOne{} minutes. Trained from scratch,
  the same model scored \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrKOneMean} and needed about
  \rqTwoMinutesKTen{} minutes (\rqTwoMinutesRecordedKTen{} as recorded) to catch up. The
  transfer model then stayed nearly flat, and with the whole practice set the model trained
  from scratch ended numerically higher (\pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfTsLrKAllMean} against
  \pct{\rqTwoRestAhSfRctTslrKAllMean}), a difference that was not tested.
  \item \textbf{What the sixth finger can and cannot be.} As a candidate ``move now''
  switch against rest, it was recognised \pct{\exFourTsLrCentralMean} of the time, window
  by window. But the same switch also turned on for \pct{\exFourTsLrCentralFiresAhMean} of
  imagined hand movements, so the data do not show a sixth-finger command that is
  distinct from the hand, and played continuously it caught only
  \pct{\exFiveFingerFingerOnMean} of the sixth-finger periods, with
  \exFiveFingerFalsePerMinMean{} false switch-ons a minute; it never ran in a closed loop. These
  numbers come from follow-up analyses added after the planned study, and they are
  labelled exploratory throughout.
  \item \textbf{A live demonstrator and website.} The trained model runs in any web
  browser on the held-out recording of one patient and moves a drawn hand with a robotic
  sixth finger. The page explains the project in figures, and every number on it is
  written from the results files.
\end{enumerate}

What we do not claim as ours: the sixth-finger idea and the dataset (Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2024jbhi,wang2026dataset}), brain-controlled limbs in general, any
clinical benefit, and live control.

\section{Organisation of the Report}\label{sec:intro-org}

Chapter~\ref{ch:lit} reviews earlier work on motor-imagery BCIs, the methods used to
decode them, ways to shorten calibration, extra robotic fingers, BCIs after stroke and
the pitfalls of testing these systems. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} describes the data, how
we checked it, the models, the leakage-free evaluation, the statistics, and the design of the
demonstrator and website. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} answers each research question in
turn, then discusses what the answers mean and where they stop.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes and lists future work. The appendices hold the
steps to reproduce every result, full tables, the models' settings, the pre-registration
and its deviations, code listings and a user guide to the demonstrator.

\section{Chapter Summary}
A sixth finger could help a weak hand grip, if brain signals can tell it when to move.
Testing that idea on a public recording raised three questions: does the published
accuracy hold without leakage, how much practice does a new patient need, and can the
sixth finger be a command of its own? The next chapter reviews what earlier work says.
